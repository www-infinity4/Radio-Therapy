Electromagnetic Waves

How oscillating fields create self-propagating waves that carry energy — and information — across space at the speed of light.

What Is an Electromagnetic Wave?

An electromagnetic (EM) wave is an oscillating disturbance in the electric and magnetic fields that propagates through space without requiring any material medium. Unlike sound waves, which are pressure waves in matter, EM waves travel happily through a perfect vacuum — in fact, that is where they travel fastest.

The electric field E and the magnetic field B are always perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel (making EM waves transverse waves). This coupling is self-sustaining: a changing electric field generates a magnetic field, and a changing magnetic field generates an electric field — the two fields feed each other forward through space.

Historical note: James Clerk Maxwell unified electricity and magnetism in his 1865 equations and predicted the existence of EM waves travelling at the speed of light. Heinrich Hertz confirmed them experimentally in 1887, producing and detecting radio waves in his laboratory.

The Fundamental Relationship

Three quantities define any EM wave: its speed c, its frequency f, and its wavelength λ. They are locked together by one of the most important equations in physics:

c = λ × f c = 299,792,458 m/s in a vacuum · λ in metres · f in Hz

This means that as frequency increases, wavelength decreases — and vice versa. A 100 MHz FM radio signal has a wavelength of about 3 metres. A 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi signal has a wavelength of about 12.5 cm. A 60 Hz power-line signal would have a wavelength of 5,000 km.

Polarisation

The polarisation of an EM wave describes the orientation of the electric field vector as the wave travels. A vertically polarised wave has its E-field pointing vertically; a horizontally polarised one points horizontally. Mismatched polarisation between transmitter and receiver antennas can reduce signal strength by 20–30 dB — a huge penalty. Circular polarisation (where the E-field rotates as it travels) avoids polarisation mismatch and is commonly used in satellite links.

Energy and Power

The power density of an EM wave (how much power flows through a unit area) is given by the Poynting vector:

S = E × H   (W/m²) S = power density · E = electric field (V/m) · H = magnetic field (A/m)

Power drops off with the square of distance in free space (the inverse-square law). Double the distance → one-quarter the power density. This is why transmitter power and antenna gain matter so much for long-distance links.

Antennas

How to convert between electrical signals and electromagnetic waves — the transducer at the heart of every radio system.

What an Antenna Does

An antenna is a transducer that converts a time-varying electrical current into a propagating electromagnetic wave (transmit mode) or captures an incoming EM wave and converts it back into an electrical current (receive mode). By the principle of reciprocity, the same antenna design works equally well for both functions.

The efficiency of this conversion, and the directions in which the antenna sends or receives energy most effectively, are the key design parameters in any radio system.

The Half-Wave Dipole: The Baseline

The simplest and most widely studied antenna is the half-wave dipole: two conductive elements, each a quarter wavelength long, fed at the centre. Its total length equals half the target wavelength — hence "half-wave".

L = (0.95 × λ) / 2 = 142.5 / fMHz metres Physical shortening factor of ~5% applied for practical wire antennas

At resonance the dipole's feedpoint impedance is approximately 73 Ω in free space — purely resistive, meaning all power is radiated (not stored). The radiation pattern is a "doughnut" shape: maximum radiation broadside to the wire, zero off the tips.

Common Antenna Types

Monopole / Whip

A quarter-wave element over a ground plane. Effectively one half of a dipole. Feedpoint impedance ≈ 35 Ω. Omnidirectional in the horizontal plane. Used in mobile radios, car antennas, and broadcast towers.

Yagi-Uda

A parasitic array: one driven dipole, one reflector (slightly longer), and multiple directors (slightly shorter). Each adds ~3–4 dB of forward gain. Used for TV reception, amateur radio, and point-to-point links.

Log-Periodic Dipole Array (LPDA)

Multiple dipoles of increasing length, all driven with alternating phase. Provides wideband coverage (many octaves) at moderate gain of 6–8 dBi. Common in spectrum-monitoring antennas.

Parabolic Dish

A reflective paraboloid focuses incident waves onto a small feed antenna at the focal point. Gain scales with dish area and frequency. Used for satellite TV (30–40 dBi) and radio telescopes (70+ dBi).

SWR and Impedance Matching

Standing-Wave Ratio (SWR) measures how well the transmission line and antenna are impedance-matched. An SWR of 1:1 is perfect — all transmitter power reaches the antenna. High SWR means energy is reflected back toward the transmitter, generating heat and potentially damaging output stages.

SWR = (1 + |Γ|) / (1 − |Γ|)   where Γ = (ZL − Z0) / (ZL + Z0) Γ = reflection coefficient · ZL = load impedance · Z0 = line impedance

An SWR of 2:1 means ~11% of power is reflected. 3:1 = ~25% reflected. Most transmitters are designed to tolerate SWR up to about 2:1 safely.

Modulation

How information is encoded onto a radio carrier wave — from simple AM to modern digital QAM.

Why Modulation Is Needed

Audio, data, or any other information signal is too low in frequency to radiate efficiently from a practical antenna. A 1 kHz audio tone would need an antenna over 150 km long to be resonant. Modulation solves this by encoding the information signal onto a much higher-frequency carrier wave, allowing it to be radiated from compact antennas and to occupy a specific, allocated slice of the radio spectrum.

Amplitude Modulation (AM)

In AM, the amplitude (height) of the carrier wave is varied in proportion to the instantaneous value of the audio or data signal. The frequency and phase of the carrier remain constant.

s(t) = [1 + m·a(t)] · Ac·cos(2πfct) m = modulation index (0–1) · a(t) = normalised audio · Ac = carrier amplitude · fc = carrier frequency

Advantages: Simple demodulation (envelope detector); compatible with crystal radios. Disadvantages: Susceptible to amplitude noise; uses twice the bandwidth needed (two sidebands) and wastes power on the unmodulated carrier (≥50% of total power carries no information).

AM broadcast uses the MF band (530–1700 kHz in North America, 153–1602 kHz in Europe) with channel spacing of 9 or 10 kHz.

Frequency Modulation (FM)

FM encodes information by varying the instantaneous frequency of the carrier, while amplitude stays constant. Because noise and interference primarily affect amplitude, FM reception is significantly cleaner than AM for the same signal level.

s(t) = Ac·cos[2πfct + 2π·kf·∫a(τ)dτ] kf = frequency deviation constant · a(τ) = modulating signal

FM broadcast uses the VHF band (87.5–108 MHz) with 200 kHz channel spacing. Peak deviation is ±75 kHz, giving a modulation index of 5 for a 15 kHz audio signal. FM stereo multiplexes a 38 kHz subcarrier.

Phase Modulation (PM) and the Digital Link

PM varies the instantaneous phase of the carrier. It is mathematically related to FM (FM is essentially the integral of PM). PM forms the foundation for modern digital modulation schemes:

  • BPSK (Binary PSK): Two phases (0°, 180°) → 1 bit per symbol. Used in deep-space comms for noise robustness.
  • QPSK (Quadrature PSK): Four phases (0°, 90°, 180°, 270°) → 2 bits per symbol. Used in satellite and cable TV.
  • 8PSK: 8 phases → 3 bits per symbol. Used in DVB-S2 satellite broadcasting.
  • 16QAM / 64QAM / 256QAM: Combines amplitude and phase variations → 4, 6, or 8 bits per symbol. Used in Wi-Fi (802.11), LTE, and cable TV.

QAM: Combining Amplitude and Phase

Quadrature Amplitude Modulation uses two carriers at the same frequency but 90° out of phase (in-phase I and quadrature Q components). Each is independently amplitude-modulated, and the receiver demodulates them separately. A 256-QAM constellation (16×16 grid of points) encodes 8 bits per symbol — the basis of modern cable internet and Wi-Fi 6.

s(t) = I(t)·cos(2πfct) − Q(t)·sin(2πfct) I = in-phase component · Q = quadrature component

Single-Sideband (SSB)

SSB removes the carrier and one sideband from an AM signal, leaving only the upper (USB) or lower (LSB) sideband. This halves the occupied bandwidth and puts all power into the information-carrying sideband — a roughly 9 dB advantage over full AM for the same intelligibility. SSB is the dominant mode in HF amateur radio and commercial HF voice communications.

Signal Propagation

How radio waves travel from transmitter to receiver — through the ground, off the sky, or straight through space.

Why Propagation Varies by Frequency

Unlike light, which we only experience as line-of-sight, radio waves interact strongly with the Earth and its atmosphere. Different frequency bands travel by fundamentally different mechanisms, which is why an AM broadcast station can be heard 1,000 km away at night while a nearby Wi-Fi router cannot penetrate a thick wall.

Ground-Wave Propagation (VLF – MF)

At low and medium frequencies, the electric field of the wave interacts with the conductive surface of the Earth, causing it to bend and follow the curvature of the planet. This ground wave propagation allows AM broadcast stations (530–1700 kHz) to cover hundreds of kilometres in the daytime without any ionospheric assistance.

Ground wave attenuation increases sharply with frequency: at 1 MHz, daytime ground-wave range over seawater might be 1,500 km; over dry land it may only be 200 km. Above 3 MHz, ground wave becomes impractical.

Sky-Wave / Ionospheric Propagation (HF)

The ionosphere — layers of the atmosphere (D, E, F1, F2) ionised by solar UV and X-rays — acts as a mirror for HF signals (3–30 MHz). Waves launched upward at the right angle are refracted back to Earth hundreds or thousands of kilometres away, enabling worldwide communication with modest power.

The maximum usable frequency (MUF) — the highest frequency that will be refracted back from the F2 layer for a given path — varies continuously with the solar cycle, time of day, and season. HF operators must tune their frequency accordingly.

Line-of-Sight (VHF and Above)

Above about 30 MHz, waves are not efficiently refracted by the ionosphere under normal conditions and travel in straight lines. The range is limited to the radio horizon — slightly beyond the optical horizon due to atmospheric refraction.

d ≈ 4.12 × (√hTX + √hRX) km h in metres. Typical FM broadcast: tower at 300 m → ~71 km LOS range

FM broadcast, television, and mobile networks all operate in this regime. Cell towers are deliberately spaced to provide overlapping LOS coverage across a region.

Free-Space Path Loss

Even in a perfect vacuum with no obstacles, signal power decreases with distance due to the spreading of the wave over an increasingly large spherical surface.

FSPL (dB) = 20·log10(d) + 20·log10(f) + 92.45 d in km · f in GHz · result in dB

A 2.4 GHz signal at 1 km has 100 dB of free-space path loss. The same signal at 10 km has 120 dB — a 100× increase in distance costs 20 dB. This is why satellite links require large dish antennas and sensitive receivers.

Multipath and Fading

In real environments, signals arrive at a receiver via multiple paths (direct, ground reflection, building reflections). When these copies arrive out of phase, they cancel (deep fade). When in phase, they reinforce. This multipath fading is the reason mobile phones can have varying signal strength even standing still. OFDM (used in Wi-Fi and LTE) combats multipath by spreading data across hundreds of narrow subcarriers, each experiencing different but manageable fading conditions.

Decibels

The logarithmic unit that makes RF engineering tractable — turning multiplications into additions.

Why Logarithms?

RF systems deal with power levels spanning 15 orders of magnitude — from a 100 W transmitter to a −130 dBm receiver noise floor. Expressing this linearly (0.000000000000001 W) is impractical. The decibel compresses this into a manageable scale where gains and losses simply add.

dB = 10 · log10(P2 / P1) For power ratios. For voltage/current: dB = 20 · log10(V2 / V1)

Absolute Reference Units

Plain dB is a ratio. Adding a suffix pins it to an absolute reference:

  • dBm: Power relative to 1 milliwatt. 0 dBm = 1 mW. A 1 W transmitter = +30 dBm. Wi-Fi typically transmits at +20 dBm (100 mW).
  • dBW: Relative to 1 watt. 0 dBW = +30 dBm.
  • dBi: Antenna gain relative to an isotropic radiator. Dipole = 2.15 dBi.
  • dBd: Antenna gain relative to a dipole. 0 dBd = 2.15 dBi.
  • dBμV/m: Field strength relative to 1 microvolt per metre. Used in EMC/EMI measurements.

The Link Budget

Because gains and losses add in dB, a complete radio link can be calculated by simply summing all contributors:

Pr = Pt + Gt − FSPL + Gr − Lcables All in dBm/dBi/dB. A positive result means the signal arrives above the noise floor.

Example: 20 dBm TX + 3 dBi TX antenna − 100 dB path loss + 3 dBi RX antenna − 2 dB cable loss = −76 dBm received power. If the receiver sensitivity is −90 dBm, we have a 14 dB link margin — a comfortable, reliable link.

Circuit Theory

Ohm's Law, power, impedance, and resonant circuits — the electrical bedrock of every RF system.

Ohm's Law

The most fundamental relationship in electrical engineering: voltage, current, and resistance are linked by Ohm's Law. In DC circuits (and for the resistive component in AC circuits):

V = I · R   ·   I = V / R   ·   R = V / I V in volts · I in amperes · R in ohms (Ω)

The three derived power equations follow directly:

P = V · I = I²R = V² / R P in watts. Use whichever form matches the known quantities.

AC Circuits and Impedance

At radio frequencies, inductors and capacitors introduce reactance — frequency-dependent opposition to current flow. Unlike resistance, reactance stores energy rather than dissipating it. The combination of resistance and reactance is impedance Z, a complex quantity:

Z = R + jX   ·   |Z| = √(R² + X²) R = resistance (Ω) · X = reactance (Ω) · j = imaginary unit
ComponentReactance formulaPhaseBehaviour at high f
ResistorX = 00°Unchanged
CapacitorXC = −1/(2πfC)−90°Approaches short circuit
InductorXL = 2πfL+90°Approaches open circuit

Resonant Circuits

When inductive and capacitive reactances are equal in magnitude they cancel, leaving only resistance. This is resonance. A parallel LC tank circuit at resonance presents maximum impedance (a "bandpass" response); a series LC presents minimum impedance. Resonant circuits are the basis of filters, oscillators, and antenna tuners.

f0 = 1 / (2π√LC) f₀ = resonant frequency (Hz) · L in henries · C in farads

The Q factor (quality factor) describes sharpness of resonance:

Q = f0 / BW = XL / R High Q → narrow bandwidth, low loss. A crystal has Q > 10,000; an air-core coil Q ≈ 100–400.

Transmission Lines

At RF frequencies, the physical length of a cable becomes significant relative to the wavelength. A transmission line (coaxial cable, microstrip, twin-lead) must be treated as a distributed circuit, characterised by its characteristic impedance Z₀ (typically 50 Ω or 75 Ω) and velocity factor (how fast signals travel relative to c in a vacuum — typically 0.66–0.85).

Signal reflections occur wherever impedance changes. Reflections create standing waves, increasing SWR and reducing the power delivered to the load. Proper impedance matching — using transformers, stubs, or matching networks — is critical in RF system design.

Skin Effect Revisited

Above a few MHz, alternating current is pushed to the outer surface of conductors by self-induced eddy currents. The characteristic depth at which current density falls to 1/e (≈37%) of its surface value is the skin depth δ:

δ = √(ρ / (π · f · μ)) ρ = resistivity (Ω·m) · f = frequency (Hz) · μ = permeability (H/m)

For copper at 100 MHz, δ ≈ 6.6 μm. At 10 GHz it's only 0.66 μm — thinner than a coat of paint. RF conductors are therefore often silver-plated (silver has lower resistivity than copper) and hollow tubing is used to save weight without sacrificing conductance.

Safety Principles

Responsible experimentation keeps you and your equipment safe.

⚠️

Electrical Safety

  • Isolate mains power before modifying circuits.
  • Use fused cables rated for your current.
  • Never work alone on high-voltage circuits.
  • Keep a Class C fire extinguisher nearby.
📻

RF & Transmitter Safety

  • Operate on licensed or licence-exempt frequencies only.
  • Maintain safe distances from transmitting antennas.
  • Never key a transmitter without a proper antenna load.
  • Check local spectrum regulations before transmitting.
🔬

Scientific Integrity

  • Record measurements with calibrated instruments.
  • Cite sources; distinguish confirmed physics from hypothesis.
  • Peer-review unusual results before publishing.
  • This project does not support mind-reading claims.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between AM and FM radio?

AM (Amplitude Modulation) encodes audio by varying the carrier's amplitude. FM (Frequency Modulation) varies the carrier's frequency. FM is more resistant to electrical noise because most interference affects amplitude, not frequency — which is why FM sounds cleaner than AM.

Why do lower-frequency signals travel farther?

Long-wave and medium-wave signals follow Earth's curvature via ground-wave propagation and refract off the ionosphere. Higher frequencies travel line-of-sight and are absorbed more readily by the atmosphere and terrain, limiting their range.

What is SWR and why does it matter?

Standing-Wave Ratio measures how well a transmission line and antenna are impedance-matched. An SWR of 1:1 is perfect. High SWR reflects energy back toward the transmitter, increasing heat, reducing range, and potentially damaging output stages.

How does a Faraday cage work?

A conductive enclosure redistributes external electric charges on its surface, cancelling the fields inside. It blocks external static and non-static electric fields. Grounded cages also dissipate incoming charges. They do not block static magnetic fields unless made from high-permeability (mu-metal) materials.

What is the skin effect and why does it matter for RF?

At high frequencies, alternating current is pushed to the outer surface of a conductor by self-induced eddy currents. This reduces the effective cross-section and increases resistance. RF engineers address it with silver-plated conductors, hollow tubes, or Litz wire.

Can I use an SDR to learn RF?

Absolutely. An RTL-SDR dongle costs under $30 and receives from ~500 kHz to 1.7 GHz. With free software (SDR#, GQRX, GNU Radio) you can explore AM/FM broadcast, aviation ADS-B, weather satellite imagery, trunked radio, and much more. It is the most accessible entry point to practical RF experimentation today.

What licences do I need to transmit on amateur radio?

Requirements vary by country. In the US the FCC issues Technician, General, and Amateur Extra licences. In the UK, Ofcom issues Foundation, Intermediate, and Full licences. All require written exams. No licence is needed to receive, or to use licence-exempt low-power ISM-band devices.

What is the Schumann Resonance?

The Schumann resonances are a set of spectrum peaks in the extremely low frequency (ELF) range of Earth's electromagnetic field spectrum, between the Earth's surface and the ionosphere. The fundamental resonance is approximately 7.83 Hz, caused by lightning discharges exciting the Earth-ionosphere cavity. It has no demonstrated effect on human cognition.
