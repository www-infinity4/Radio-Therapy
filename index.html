RF Theory

Grounded in established physics. Every formula below is experimentally verified.

Electromagnetic Waves

Radio waves are a form of electromagnetic (EM) radiation—oscillating electric and magnetic fields that propagate through space at the speed of light. They were predicted by James Clerk Maxwell in 1865 and first experimentally demonstrated by Heinrich Hertz in 1887.

Unlike mechanical waves, EM waves require no medium; they travel freely through a vacuum. The electric field (E) and magnetic field (B) are always perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel.

c = λ · f Speed of light = wavelength × frequency

The Electromagnetic Spectrum

EM radiation spans an enormous range of frequencies. Radio waves occupy the lowest-frequency, longest-wavelength end (3 Hz – 300 GHz), while gamma rays sit at the opposite extreme (> 1019 Hz).

Antennas and Resonance

An antenna converts electrical energy into EM waves (transmit) or EM waves into electrical energy (receive). A half-wave dipole—the simplest resonant antenna—has a physical length equal to half the wavelength of its target frequency.

L = 143 / fMHz (metres) Half-wave dipole length (using 5% velocity factor)

At resonance the antenna's impedance is purely resistive (~73 Ω for a dipole in free space), maximising power transfer and minimising standing-wave ratio (SWR).

Modulation

A carrier wave alone carries no information. Modulation encodes a signal by varying one property of the carrier:

  • AM (Amplitude Modulation) — varies carrier amplitude; susceptible to noise.
  • FM (Frequency Modulation) — varies carrier frequency; more noise-resistant.
  • PM (Phase Modulation) — varies carrier phase; basis for modern digital comms.
  • QAM — combines AM and PM for high spectral efficiency (used in Wi-Fi, LTE).

Decibels (dB)

The decibel is a logarithmic unit expressing the ratio between two power levels. It compresses the enormous dynamic range of RF systems into manageable numbers.

dB = 10 · log10(Pout / Pin) Power ratio in decibels

A +3 dB gain ≈ doubling of power. A −10 dB loss = one-tenth the power. Cascaded gains and losses simply add in dB form—one of the primary reasons the unit is so widely used.

Ohm's Law & Power

The foundational relationship between voltage (V), current (I), and resistance (R) underlies all circuit design.

V = I · R   ·   P = V · I = I²R = V²/R Ohm's Law and DC power equations

In AC circuits, reactive components (capacitors, inductors) introduce phase shifts and impedance (Z) replaces pure resistance, but the same ratios apply using RMS values.

Glossary

Key terms every RF experimenter should know.

Amplitude
The peak value of a waveform, representing signal strength or power.
Antenna
A transducer that converts between electrical signals and electromagnetic waves.
Bandwidth
The range of frequencies a system can process; wider bandwidth = more data capacity.
Carrier Wave
A continuous-wave signal that is modulated to carry information.
dBm
Power level in dB referenced to 1 milliwatt. 0 dBm = 1 mW, 30 dBm = 1 W.
Decibel (dB)
A logarithmic unit expressing the ratio of two power or voltage values.
Demodulation
Extracting the original information signal from a modulated carrier.
Dipole
The simplest antenna: two conductive elements forming a resonant half-wave radiator.
Electromagnetic (EM) Wave
Coupled oscillating electric and magnetic fields that propagate through space at c.
Frequency
The number of oscillation cycles per second; measured in Hertz (Hz).
Gain
The ratio of output power to input power; a positive value means amplification.
Ground Plane
A conductive surface used as an electrical reference and reflector in antenna design.
Impedance (Z)
Opposition to AC current flow, combining resistance and reactance. Measured in Ω.
Modulation
Encoding information by varying a property (amplitude, frequency, or phase) of a carrier.
Noise Floor
The level of background electrical noise limiting the weakest detectable signal.
Ohm's Law
V = IR; the relationship between voltage, current, and resistance in a linear circuit.
Propagation
How radio waves travel from transmitter to receiver—through ground, sky, or space.
Resonance
Condition where a circuit's inductive and capacitive reactances cancel, giving pure resistance.
Skin Effect
At high frequencies, current concentrates near a conductor's surface, increasing effective resistance.
Wavelength (λ)
The physical distance one full cycle of a wave travels; λ = c / f.

Safety Principles

Responsible experimentation keeps you safe and your equipment working.

⚠️

Electrical Safety

  • Always isolate mains power before modifying circuits.
  • Use fused cables rated for your current.
  • Never work alone on high-voltage circuits.
  • Keep a fire extinguisher rated for electrical fires nearby.
📻

RF & Transmitter Safety

  • Operate only on licensed frequencies or inside licence-exempt power limits.
  • Maintain safe distances from transmitting antennas (EIRP tables apply).
  • Never key a transmitter without a proper antenna load.
  • Check local spectrum regulations before transmitting.
🔬

Scientific Integrity

  • Record measurements with calibrated instruments.
  • Cite sources and distinguish confirmed physics from hypothesis.
  • Peer-review unusual results before publishing.
  • This project does not support mind-reading or behaviour-manipulation claims.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between AM and FM radio?

AM (Amplitude Modulation) encodes audio by varying the carrier's amplitude. FM (Frequency Modulation) varies the carrier's frequency instead. FM is more resistant to electrical noise because most interference affects amplitude, not frequency—which is why FM audio sounds cleaner than AM.

Why do lower-frequency signals travel farther?

Long-wave and medium-wave signals (below ~3 MHz) follow the curvature of the Earth via ground-wave propagation and can also refract off the ionosphere (sky-wave). Higher frequencies travel in straight lines (line-of-sight) and are absorbed more readily by the atmosphere and terrain, limiting their range.

What is SWR and why does it matter?

Standing-Wave Ratio (SWR) measures how well a transmission line and antenna are impedance-matched. An SWR of 1:1 is perfect — all power reaches the antenna. High SWR means energy is reflected back toward the transmitter, increasing heat, reducing range, and potentially damaging transmitter output stages.

How does a Faraday cage work?

A Faraday cage is a conductive enclosure that redistributes external electric charges on its surface, cancelling out the fields inside. It blocks external static and non-static electric fields. Grounded cages also dissipate incoming charges. However, they do not block magnetic fields unless made from high-permeability materials.

What is the skin effect and why does it matter for RF?

At high frequencies, alternating current is pushed to the outer surface of a conductor by self-induced eddy currents. This reduces the effective cross-section, increasing resistance. RF engineers address it by using silver-plated conductors, hollow tubes, or Litz wire to maximise the surface area carrying current.

What licences do I need to transmit on amateur radio?

Requirements vary by country. In the US, the FCC issues Technician, General, and Amateur Extra licences. In the UK, Ofcom issues Foundation, Intermediate, and Full licences. All require passing written examinations. No licence is needed to receive, or to use licence-exempt low-power devices (e.g. Bluetooth, ISM band).

Can I use an SDR (Software-Defined Radio) to learn RF?

Absolutely. An RTL-SDR dongle costs under $30 and lets you receive signals from ~500 kHz to 1.7 GHz. Combined with free software like SDR#, GQRX, or GNU Radio, you can explore AM/FM broadcast, aviation ADS-B, weather satellite imagery, and much more. It is the most accessible way to start practical RF experimentation today.
